# Anti-slop checklist

Slop is writing that could have been produced without the source material. It sounds fluent, commits to nothing and belongs to no one. Run every draft through the tests below before delivery. A draft that fails one test is not finished.

The author's own vocabulary wins over any generic ban. If a phrase appears in his source posts, it is his and stays available. If a phrase appears only in this list, it stays out.

## Phrases that mark a post as machine written

The author has publicly said he hates these openers when they appear in AI comments on his posts. They are banned in his copy in any position:

- "The interesting part is..."
- "X is the real story here"
- "The real shift here is..." / "The real takeaway here is..."

Also banned by the user's instruction, even though the author has used it: "To set the stage" and "To Set The Stage:" as a section opener. It became a tic across drafts. Go straight into the first fact, or use a plain "Here's what happened:".

Also banned, because they add no information and readers now recognize them on sight:

- "Here's the thing" / "Here's the truth" / "Harsh truth:" / "Unpopular opinion:"
- "Let that sink in" / "Read that again" / "I'll say it louder"
- The negation pivot in every form: "It's not about X. It's about Y.", "Not because X. Because Y.", "The problem isn't X. It's Y.", "This isn't X, it's Y." A draft in this repository failed on "Not because it's bad. Because the output is a gigantic meh." The first half was filler that existed only to set up the second. Say the second half on its own. The author's one-line "The ultimate goal of starting a company isn't to be rich, it's to bow to no one" is the exception that proves the rule: both halves are concrete and the second is a surprise. If a draft's pivot is not that, cut the negation.
- "Game-changer", "unlock", "leverage" as a verb, "empower", "navigate", "landscape", "journey", "resonate", "authentic", "north star", "double down", "delve", "seamless", "robust", "elevate"
- "In today's fast-paced world" / "In a world where..." as an opener
- "Most people won't tell you this" / "Nobody talks about this"
- "This is huge." / "This changes everything." / "Big if true."
- "Stop doing X. Start doing Y." as a closing formula
- "Lesson:" or "Takeaway:" followed by a slogan instead of a lesson
- "Let's continue the conversation in the comments" / "What's your take?" / "Agree?" as the close. The author's "Did I miss anything?" after a list the reader can add to is his and stays.

## Structural slop

- **Hook that summarizes.** The first line tells the reader what the post will conclude. Enter the scene or the contradiction instead.
- **Body that restates the hook.** The second paragraph says the first paragraph again with more words.
- **The moral three times.** The lesson appears mid-post, at the end, and again as a sign-off. Say it once, where it lands.
- **Metronome paragraphs.** Every paragraph exactly one sentence. The author mixes long, run-on rant paragraphs with one-line story beats. Match the paragraph to its job.
- **Zinger tax.** Every paragraph ends with a one-line punch. After the third, none of them land.
- **Abstract list.** Numbered items that are categories ("Mindset", "Consistency", "Systems") rather than things that happened or things to do.
- **Symmetry for its own sake.** Three parallel clauses, three adjectives, three examples, every time. Real material is lumpy.
- **Rhetorical question chains.** Two or more questions in a row that the writer then answers.
- **Assembled sentences.** Fragments joined by dashes or built as "Label: explanation" where a written sentence would do. The author's own dash asides, "TAKEAWAY:" headers and "Me:" dialogue labels are his and stay.
- **Emoji bullets, hashtags, bold on every line, thread markers.** The author uses an occasional emoji or arrow, not a formatting system.

## Fabrication slop

- A founder who "DM'd me yesterday" who did not.
- Dialogue reconstructed from nothing and presented as verbatim.
- A number rounded up, moved in time, or combined from two companies. Check `identity/proof.md`.
- A composite anecdote presented as one event.
- A feeling the source does not support, added for vulnerability. "I was terrified" is not garnish.
- A guest's result restated as the author's credibility.
- A forecast written as a milestone, or a target written as a result.

## Tests to run on every draft

1. **Delete test.** Remove each paragraph in turn. If the post loses nothing, the paragraph goes.
2. **Swap test.** Replace the author's company names, numbers and names with a competitor's. If the post still reads fine, it is generic. Put the specifics back in and rebuild around them.
3. **Source test.** Every claim, quote, date and number points to a file in the repository or to the brief. Anything that does not is marked as illustrative in the delivery note or removed.
4. **Stranger test.** A reader who has never heard of the author should understand the stakes from the copy alone.
5. **Dinner test.** Would the author say this sentence across a table to a founder friend? If it sounds like a keynote or a press release, rewrite it as speech.
6. **Read-aloud test.** Read the whole post aloud. Stumbles mark sentences that were assembled rather than written.
7. **Truncation test.** Cut the post at 140 characters. Does the visible fragment still make someone click?
8. **Phrase scan.** Search the draft for every phrase in the banned list above. Zero hits.

## What good looks like instead

- One tension, one lesson, one reader.
- Specifics from the source: the dated event, the exact figure, the actual line someone said.
- Emotion that the source supports, stated plainly, with the caveat beside the claim.
- Paragraph length that follows the job: long when arguing, short when landing.
- A close that gives the reader a feeling, in the author's own repeated words where he actually repeats them.
