Embracing AI to Transform Career and Work

Robb Clarke
8mo
Report this post
2025 was a friggin' wild year.

I've been thinking a lot about the changes that
RB2B
went through and really, how AI has impacted my career growth over the last year. I went from Technical Operations Manager to Head of AI this past summer. 

But you know what? AI didn't change my job title.

It changed the direction of my career.

Most of my career has been built by saying yes before I had everything figured out. Front-end development. Customer work. Product decisions. I learned by doing, then kept moving forward.

AI followed the same pattern.

I didn't start by trying to overhaul everything. I started with the work I dreaded most. The repetitive support questions. The tedious spreadsheets. The tasks that ate time without adding much value. I trained AI to handle those first.

That shift gave me something more important than efficiency.

It gave me time.

When we brought on
Fin
, support was largely handled, I could focus on product, testing, and improving how people actually experience what we build. My role moved away from reacting to problems and toward preventing them altogether. 

Support became less about answering tickets and more about managing knowledge and improving the system behind it.

AI didn't replace my work. It widened it.

I’m now able to go deeper into areas I didn't formally come from, without burning out or stretching myself thin. The human parts of the job matter more. The thinking, the judgment, the decisions.

The biggest change wasn't technical.

It was how I approached the tool.

AI works best when you treat it like something you can learn alongside, not something you need to fear. I trained it, and in the process, it trained me. It filled gaps, saved time, and made me better at the work I already cared about.

My advice to anyone unsure where to start is simple.

Find the most painful part of your job and let AI take a swing at it.

Say yes.

Then figure it out.

When the online commerce industry kickstarted slowly in the late 90s and gained traction super linearly in the 2000s, one big limiting factor for businesses was employee attrition. Other than doctors, and other emergency services, very few professionals are acquainted with the term Oncall. 

“Keep the lights on”. Increasingly, as the shift left philosophy was and is being adopted with automation replacing process with humans involved, people learnt through post mortems, human intervention is most of the time necessary as part of the lifecycle of software development operations. Given a certain size of business and “work”, this basically results in what I and people who have researched this called “extrinsic cognitive load”.

“Hey, I have a PR, can you review it?”
“Hey, Our order rate metric is below the predicted line, investigate please. By which I mean, now”.

… fast forward to 2024-2025. AI agentic workflows may be getting into an era where this is going to follow the Gartner curve across the spectrum of Software Delivery Operations (SDO). Agents will wreak havoc (at times), before learnings and Software Development processes and patterns adapt to working with these agents. HITL will not go away for many many years to come. Very attractive at demos, but when the rubber meets the road, shit gets real sometimes. It’s like Newtonian Physics having a beer with Einstein’s Relativity Physics close to the Sun.

In the meantime, we will have Organizations shedding open recs (usually less experienced developers), more money for PagerDuty, psychiatric counseling, rage quitting etc. VPs and Directors better be prepared for this. And CTOs especially be very very wary of this pattern developing. Especially when chatting with the former pool of Engineering leaders.

ps: It’s easy to please Product and the company’s bottom line in the short term. The medium term ramifications of every agentic workflow in your SDO lifecycle needs to have careful guardrails.

Most people don’t know this about me.

I work in customer service.

And strangely, that role changed how I think about Data, Finance, and even Myself.

Before now, if an app didn’t work for me, or a website confused me, or a product didn’t deliver…

I would just leave.

I just assumed maybe it’s not for me.
Maybe I’m not lucky.
Maybe that’s just how it is.

But working in customer service rewired that mindset.

Every day, people reach out to us.
Not because they’re weak.
Not because they’re wrong.

But because they expect BETTER.

And we listen, We investigate, We escalate and We fix their issues.

That’s when it clicked.

If people can speak up for products they didn’t build,
why was I quietly accepting friction in tools I wanted to use?🤔

So I started doing something different.

Anytime I didn’t understand an app, anytime a process confused me or something didn’t work the way it should…

I reached out.

whether it's Instagram, Live chat, Support emails or official websites.

I started reaching out and guess what?

They responded, they explained the issue and they helped.

That experience quietly changed how I approached everything else.

In finance, instead of walking away confused, I asked questions.
In tech, instead of feeling behind, I leaned into learning.
In data, instead of guessing, I looked for patterns and clarity.

Customer service taught me something simple but powerful:

You don’t have to stay silent just because something feels unfamiliar.
You’re allowed to ask.
You’re allowed to learn.
You’re allowed to demand clarity.

That mindset is now how I approach data, finance, and growth.

Not rushing.
Not pretending.
Just understanding step by step.

And honestly?

That shift matters more to me than speed.

I’m going to show you the move that turns a reactive, figure it out career into a proactive one people actually hire for and pay for.

Most figure it out people describe themselves like a personality trait:
• adaptable
• scrappy
• can wear many hats

That used to work.

In the AI era, it makes you invisible.

The fix:

You don’t stop being a figure it out person.
You package it into a role the market already understands.

The formula:

Ops lane + domain edge + AI forward execution = leverage

Here’s one example, then you can apply it to yourself.

Example: Product Ops

Say you’ve spent years product adjacent:
• coordinating launches
• building workflows
• fixing systems
• making teams faster behind the scenes

You’re not trying to jump straight to Product Manager.

You’re trying to get into the product org in a way that fits how your brain works.

That door is often Product Ops.

Here’s the part most people miss.

Lean into your background

Let’s say your background was in manufacturing or technical design:
• training on complex systems
• working with tooling or specs
• managing vendors, compliance, or handoffs

Even if none of that said “product” on paper.

Stack that on top of Product Ops and you stop looking generic and start looking rare.

“I want to work in product.”

But:
“I do Product Ops for complex systems, and I am AI first about how I do it.”

Different framing.

1. Name your domain edge
Manufacturing. Insurance. Healthcare. Finance. Education. Logistics. Whatever you have actually lived inside.

AI first workflows. Automation by default. Systems that make small teams unfairly effective.

I'm starting to believe that customer proximity matters even more than technical skill when it comes to AI adoption. Here's why.
 
Believe it or not, the people having the most success aren't the most technical. Instead, it's those closest to customers.
 
I have a client right now who has a tech background, but definitely isn't a programmer. But with these tools, he's producing highly valuable code with no security holes. He managed to do 60 pull requests in 2 weeks.
 
On the other hand, the engineers I'm working with aren't seeing the same results. And the further away someone is from the customer, the more difficult it becomes.
 
I think this stems from the fact that AI doesn't know what solves a customer problem. It's really good at going from A to B when you tell it what success looks like. But that means the person behind the wheel has to know where they want to go.
 
And that's where proximity comes in. The person in the thick of it with customers sees the problems every day, so their brain can easily generate tasks based on their real-world experiences.
 
Engineers just haven't had the need or the opportunity to build this intuition. They've always relied on others to tell them what to build. Without a direct connection to customer problems, they're stuck waiting for someone to hand them a task.
 
And that's why (for the time being, at least), the more someone is right there with the customer and their problems, the more effectively they can put these tools to use.
