## September 4: Adam, Mark Kosoglow and moderator Pete Crowley

**Attribution.** The description names Mark; Pete introduces himself as RB2B's head of growth and acknowledges that his employer pays him. Adam identifies himself as RB2B founder/CEO. Turns are recoverable through moderator questions and direct address despite the absence of formal diarization. Mark's former commission-only work is his personal business context, not a bootstrapped SaaS founded by Adam. [0:00–8:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cxke-F1XL3w)

**Funding and personal circumstances, 5:00–15:59.** Mark describes moving from hiring only when sellers were overwhelmed to hiring ahead of capacity as investor expectations changed. He says family expenses meant he could not afford years without income. Adam accepts that stage of life and size of idea affect the decision. He says his public posts often address his earlier self and advocate for early-stage founders; short-form absolutes leave out that frame. Adam's underlying concern is the founder's realized outcome and control, including needing board approval for compensation. Mark adds that building a business and making personal wealth are different skills. His Catalyst growth claim remains guest self-report, not Adam-company proof.

**Distribution debate, 15:00–28:59.** Adam promotes inbound-led outbound and the commercial advantage of category naming, while acknowledging that novelty is not necessary to the positioning effort. Mark objects to renaming familiar marketing and indiscriminate automated follow-up. Adam responds with an approximately 10% monthly RB2B churn self-report, admitting neither customers nor his team yet fully understands the best usage pattern. Churn type/window beyond monthly is unspecified. He describes strong enthusiasm among very small monitoring users and sophisticated larger growth teams, with a less satisfying middle segment. He explicitly distinguishes impressive automation content from a workflow a small enterprise team should actually run. These points support stage- and context-dependent operating choices, not a permanent ban on automation.

**Founder work and risk, 29:00–39:59.** The moderator defines Adam's zero-to-one as first $1m ARR and compares it with Mark's sales-role trajectory to $250m ARR. This is a debate prompt, not a claim that Adam ran a $250m business. Mark recounts his own commission sales and family schedule. Adam identifies more strongly with early product creation and the lean zero-to-ten phase, and says he wants to diversify away from high-risk businesses. His roughly 55-person Retention.com reference and recent downsizing are historical staffing statements, without FTE/contractor allocation. Adam recalls savings, being single, a bad career move and getting fired before deciding to try entrepreneurship with finance as a fallback. His later 2025 account says he quit finance in 2011; these are variant departure descriptions, not a resolved employment chronology.

**Founder brand, 40:00–49:59.** Adam thinks strong content can continue to work despite saturation, conditioned on buyers using the platform and founders committing to the craft. Mark's roughly 20-minute daily writing practice and reported 20 meetings in two hours belong to Mark. General claims about algorithms detecting AI are unsourced opinions. These sections were read fully but not expanded in the financing page.

**Liquidity and purpose, 49:00–59:41.** Adam speculates that VC fund structures cannot accommodate dividends; Mark describes power-law incentives. Neither provides fund documents or a general legal analysis. Do not turn the exchange into a universal prohibition. Adam contrasts an investor's portfolio with a founder's single decade-long effort, and wants the profitable smaller-company option to receive more attention. His discussion of odds is advocacy, not a statistical study. The closing's claims about a future Chili Piper guest are promotional third-party biography and were not treated as current facts.

## September 25: Amit Vasudev and Santosh; Adam absent

**Decisive boundary, 1:00–1:59.** The host explicitly states Adam is not on the call. The introductory $150m Clearbit acquisition value comes from host framing/description. Amit later discusses the sale but does not independently establish that purchase price. No point in this episode becomes an Adam belief merely because it appears on his channel. The guest's name is supplied by the description; caption spellings of his name, Earl Grey Capital and other proper nouns vary.

**Lean beginnings and buyer choice, 4:00–20:59.** Amit says Clearbit began in 2015, initially targeted developers and shifted toward marketing use cases with higher willingness to pay. He credits a goal of profitability around $70k MRR despite early institutional funding. His chronology describes $1m ARR at the end of 2015, $3.3m the following year, then above $8m. The three-rep efficiency example does not mean three total company employees. Senior technical generalists, founders, free entry and an enterprise-style buying process all contributed. His criticism is of choosing PLG or another motion ideologically before understanding how customers buy. SEO and listicle tactics are historical observations, not current search recommendations.

**Acquisition, 21:00–26:59.** Amit credits CEO Matt with leading the HubSpot transaction. He says Clearbit had about $40m ARR and frames strategic value, relationships, team and product fit as more relevant to the buyer than the latest funding valuation. The comparison to HubSpot's scale is rhetorical, not audited buyer economics. He had earlier expected a PE outcome and says strategic deals are difficult to manufacture. Macro sensitivity and financing pressure are retrospective lessons; no exact transaction waterfall is supplied.

**Labor and product constraints, 27:00–37:59.** Amit favors founders staying close to hiring and gives an example of one full-time growth leader supported by about 16 contractors. Headcount alone would miss those inputs. He says he and Alex stepped away at the end of 2020; this is guest history. His central regret is selecting a data product with naturally episodic value and then trying to make it fit recurring-software growth expectations. He distinguishes data consumed heavily early in a relationship from products that accumulate value and customer dependence. Some Clearbit products were more recurring than others; do not flatten the whole company into a single retention profile.

**Venture reasoning, 38:00–47:59.** The title's anti-VC framing omits Amit's explicit defense of risk capital's social usefulness. He says founders should understand private-market illiquidity, portfolio return incentives and the role of investor signaling. His questions include whether the idea could attain venture scale, whether the founder accepts the obligation to return capital, and whether money accelerates something already working. His then-current IPO size benchmarks are estimates, not listing rules. A numerical ownership comparison is unreliable as spoken: 80% of a $100m sale is $80m, while 5% of a $2bn sale is $100m, before transaction adjustments; the transcript claims the former is larger. Preserve the point about ownership without reusing that arithmetic.

**Later-stage debate and close, 48:00–1:01:17.** Amit explains his fund's then-stated preference for developer/API/platform businesses over some crowded application categories. He argues lower product-building costs and tougher distribution weaken the old raise-and-hire path. He proposes inexpensive initial value followed by additional product value, and hopes capital shifts toward more ambitious technical problems. These are a historical investor thesis and suggestions. No current fund mandate, investment eligibility or present competitor status was verified.

**Early funding story, 0:00–7:59.** Nathan describes his first startup, hiring to win social validation, a $2m round at an $8.5m pre-money valuation, a later COO grant and his resulting roughly 38% stake. These numbers do not reconstruct the whole cap table. He says investors blocked an early acquisition he wanted. Spoken context suggests a $6.5m offer, but the automatic text also renders “$6,500”; no slide inspection or deal document was performed. His tax/t-bill illustration is hypothetical, not a verified historical return. Adam asks about the loss of flexibility and relates to the excitement of a potential exit. He does not claim this was his own blocked transaction. Around 3:00, he affirms Retention.com and RB2B are bootstrapped.

**Media and acquisition economics, 8:00–23:59.** Adam credits Nathan's interviews with helping him understand other startup metrics, and says he wants his own efficient one-to-many selling motion. Nathan describes building a durable media asset, using it to build owned audiences and using those audiences to support further assets and customers. He reports selling slide decks to cover studio costs. That is his cost-recovery example, not audited fully loaded CAC and not RB2B revenue. Adam's enthusiasm and reported experimentation support adoption of the principle, not proof he achieved Nathan's result.

**Deal terms and comparison, 24:00–32:59.** Adam reads a hypothetical $500m exit example; Nathan explains preferences, participation and delayed/stock proceeds. This is educational rhetoric, not an identified actual Salesforce acquisition. Captions say 90% of $120m is locked up and then give $96m: 90% would be $108m, leaving $12m. The source mixes or misstates a number, and the simplified allocation/tax assumptions do not support a reliable founder-payout calculation. Nathan also discusses Pry/Brex with caveats about details he cannot share. No transaction documentation was examined. Preferred versus participating terminology is loose; do not promote it into legal drafting advice.

Adam then frames a lean $10m ARR business as a desirable alternative, with possible recurring distributions, a smaller buyer universe threshold and optional future choices. His claimed minimum sale prices, buyer counts and instant access to PE financing are unsupported generalizations. These are distinct from his own achieved financial results.

**Compensation and founder outcomes, 33:00–42:59.** Adam recounts an unnamed peer group's compensation discussion and reads salary-table figures from slides. Neither the data set nor its sample was independently examined; leave the people unnamed. He contrasts growth's valuation premium with the uncertain transaction required to realize it. Nathan shows a DocHub example and expressly says the P&L is **not Adam's**. DocHub's claimed $500k CEO adjustment and sale offers must not enter Adam's proof. Nathan's illustrative 10%-of-1,000 funding statement also uses 990 as the complement; the arithmetic is inconsistent, and the underlying probability is not sourced.

**Founderpath, 43:00–50:59.** Adam says he connected Stripe, inspected his metrics and liked the additional valuation analysis. This establishes a product exploration, not borrowing. Nathan contrasts starting MRR, new MRR and churn; he then switches to his own GetLatka account. Its reported revenue decline is not an RB2B decline. His $150m fund-size claim, ContactMonkey secondary story and other customer transactions remain attributed historical guest examples. His approximate $500k ARR debt threshold is explicitly conditioned on a working way to deploy capital. No loan terms, suitability assessment or current product eligibility is established.

**Distribution close, 51:00–57:04.** Nathan reports historical organic clicks and uses an assumed paid-click price to illustrate value; this is not measured ad-spend savings. His SMS result specifies a sent-message denominator, unlike the email open/click comparison. Adam closes by promoting his developing webinar presentation. These sections were fully read but do not establish financing events.
