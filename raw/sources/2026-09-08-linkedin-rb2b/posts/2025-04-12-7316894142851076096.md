## Exact post text

The HARDEST thing about being a founder is that you need to maintain absolute conviction, while keeping an open mind that you might be 100% wrong. Last quarter, I bet RB2B’s entire future on a new idea. “This is it. This is how we’ll get to $20M ARR. Nobody else is doing it.” Then two months later, I abandoned it completely. Here’s what happened:

RB2B churn is mizz.

It needs to be something very different than it is to grow past $5m ARR.

Nick Weldon (Founder of 5x5) and I came up with the idea of always-on display ad product for SMB B2Bs.

AI-generated ads that marketers KNEW were being shown to rep’s target accounts. 

Kind of like a person-level Demandbase for the little guy.

We spent all morning on it, and celebrated with some steak tacos. 

I scootered back to my office and could hardly contain my excitement.

“THIS is going to be my path to $20m ARR," I told myself.

I spent the next week mapping out the details of what I was trying to create. 

MORE importantly, I made a list of people to speak to who were EXPERTS on the ads ecosystem. 

The biggest names out there. 

Gabe, the CEO of Demandbase. Chris Walker. Dimitri, CEO of Influ2. Gil, CEO of Metadata.

I was flying high into my first conversation, which was with Chris Walker. 

I pitched him my entire walk home from my office, and as I walked into my back yard, I finished, and waited for him to respond.

There was an uncomfortable pause.

“I really just DON’T like the idea at all.” 

I felt like I got punched in the gut. 

I respect Chris’s opinion A LOT, but he’s not the end-all-be-all, so I went on with my calls the next two weeks. 

What happened?

I have NEVER had that universally NEGATIVE feedback about a product idea.

––––

So, I decided to kill the idea.

The same idea that was my ENTIRE WORLD for the prior month or so. 

All my hope, all my inspiration, all my dreams.

Here’s the crazy thing… I felt real loss when I killed it.

A light sadness in my chest for about the next week or so… As if a thing I had before was gone.

I had internalized the success of the product - and what it would have added to my life - even before we started building anything. 

Startups are so hard. 

I think you have to internalize success before you start to have CLOSE to enough energy to do what it takes to get something off the ground.

But at the same time, you have to be willing to change your mind completely if the world tells you it doesn’t want what you’re making.

Average founders aren't willing to accept that nobody wants what they’re building. 

They push out a product nobody wants, and their entire life is a grind. 

The best founders do the opposite. 

Strong convictions, loosely held. 

And you just keep swinging.
